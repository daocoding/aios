# Lecture evidence: the practitioner layer

*The numbers, cases and decision rules from CC's 2026-09-12 lecture that back up [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md), in our own words. Times are on the lecture's single 2h17m clock; "slide" means the figure appears on CC's slide, "spoken" means he said it. Local-currency amounts are converted at ~7 per USD and rounded. These are one operator's observations from one platform at one point in time: use them as benchmarks to test against, not as guarantees. aiOS defaults are listed separately in METHODOLOGY §10.*

## 1. Why expand into a new market at all
| Evidence | Source |
|---|---|
| A crowded home market develops a **price floor**: someone is always cheaper, and below a certain price marketing stops mattering. Sellers who survived that bring a strong supply chain and **resilience** that is an edge abroad. | spoken 0:00:53 |
| AI has levelled two old barriers: not knowing the country (information access) and not being able to make video. | spoken 0:00:01–0:01:25 |
| Entering a new market is a **second startup**: every factor is recombined and the team must be rebuilt. 70–80% of CC's team have lived abroad, because local cultural instinct has to be in the team. | spoken 0:13:50 |
| Wrong stocking decisions cost students from hundreds of thousands to over a million; "avoiding pitfalls is making money." | spoken 0:21:44 |
| **Three batches in motion** (one selling in the destination warehouse, one at sea, one in production) tie up cash, which is why blind stocking is so dangerous. | spoken 0:11:41 |
| Before stocking, CC's standard is to sample around 500 creators across audience groups to check that demand is real. | spoken 0:12:06 |

## 3. Unit economics
| Evidence | Source |
|---|---|
| Cost stack: price − (unit cost + first/last-mile logistics + warehousing + platform fees + ads + returns). | slide 0:56:34 |
| Worked example: $29.99 price, $3.57 unit cost, $6.00 logistics, payout fee 6% + $0.30 = $2.10 (= 7.00% platform fixed-fee share), 31.92% landed, 38.92% fixed, 5% refunds → **56.08% gross**; 30% creators + ads → **26.08% company margin** (54.76 in home currency). | slide 0:56:34, spoken 0:56:19 |
| Back-solve competitors' cost from their price to see how much "ammunition" each side has; you don't need to match their price. | slide 0:57:24 |
| Short video can explain a differentiated value in 20 seconds, so it builds a brand; marketplace (shelf) selling only moves goods. | spoken 0:57:20 |

## 4. Creators
| Evidence | Source |
|---|---|
| A sample costs about **$14** landed (product + both shipping legs); 100 samples ≈ **$1,400**; typically only **5–8 of 100** creators' videos sell. It loses money on paper but is far cheaper than pre-stocking **2,000 units**. | spoken 1:11:33–1:12:05 |
| Owner metrics: sample video return ≥90%; re-booking/conversion 50–55%. | slide 1:17:38 |
| CC hires **experienced trade/export salespeople** for creator partnerships: SOPs teach the platform in half a day; communication can't be taught that fast. | spoken 1:14:57 |
| With no US staff, CC briefed **local students** to meet creators face to face; "every long-term creator you sign is like a new distributor." | spoken 1:16:00 |

## 5. Content
| Evidence | Source |
|---|---|
| Beats: A 0–3s stop · B 3–11s believe · C 11–15s act. | slide 1:34:53 |
| **First-6-second gate:** if ~60 of 100 viewers watch through second 6, the video is pushed to the next traffic pool. | spoken 1:32:05 |
| An explicit "tap to buy" instruction changed conversion by about **3%** in CC's tests. | spoken 1:32:31 |
| Same teeth-whitening script: shot in the bathroom with emotion it sold; shot flat in a living room it read as an ad and flopped. **Emotion** is a core principle. | spoken 1:24:02 |
| **99% / 1%:** 99% of selling hits follow the standard framework; 1% are personality or meme hits you can't replicate (a meme video that sold 10,000 orders with no selling point). | spoken 1:28:01–1:29:39 |
| Owners who go to training alone can't transfer it: bring the hands-on team. | spoken 1:28:01 |
| Selling points: one per week (ch. 1, 0:37:18) and two per week (ch. 3, 1:37:42) both appear; grade them S/A/B/C. | spoken; slide 1:38:13 |
| An editor writes ~5 scripts a day; AI gives 50 directions in 5 seconds. | slide 1:41:33 |
| **AI video at scale:** 1:1 remakes of winners (swap product/outfit, identical motion); seven creators licensed their likeness for revenue share; synthetic presenters with their own accounts now get brand deals; a ~20-person AI-video team with unattended overnight editing; one intern runs ~30 accounts at **$80–100k/month**. | slides 1:41:37–1:45:33, spoken 1:41:37–1:42:22 |

## 6. Paid
| Evidence | Source |
|---|---|
| 0→1: define the **break-even ROI line and the stop-loss line**; aim for a replicable conversion signal, not immediate profit. | slide 1:50:03 |
| Case: $15 bracelet, ROI target above break-even → unstable, starved delivery. | slide 1:50:48 |
| **Spike playbook:** same day, review the high-converting creative, add budget to test similar audiences, catch traffic with similar products and creator follow-ups. "Copy the win rate, not the budget." | slide 1:51:53 |
| 100 orders a day needs 200+ creatives. | spoken 1:53:40 |
| **Ceiling test** (Thailand consumer electronics): watch marginal acquisition cost, conversion, creative freshness, audience expansion, inventory turnover; if profit from new revenue keeps falling as budget rises, add creatives, improve the product or open a market instead of pushing. | slide 1:53:48 |
| Ad-diagnosis demo: 46 creatives, 6 delivering (≈13% activation), ROI 1.8 vs break-even 2.6. | slides 1:56:17 onward |
